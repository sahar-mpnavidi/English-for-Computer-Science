# Lesson 23: AR/VR, Metaverse, and Immersive Tech

## Level: B2–C1
## Time: 90–120 minutes

> **درس ۲۳ — واقعیت افزوده و مجازی، متاورس و فناوری غوطه‌ور**
> **نکتهٔ گرامری:** شرطی‌های ترکیبی (mixed conditionals) · گذشتهٔ خیالی با نتیجهٔ حال، و حال خیالی با نتیجهٔ گذشته
> **متن درس:** ۲۹۱۵ واژه (۳۳ پاراگراف) | **واژگان کلیدی:** ۱۲۴ واژه | **باند سطح:** B2–C1 (پیشرفته)

---

### 1. Lesson Objectives / اهداف درس

- تفاوت واقعیت مجازی، افزوده، ترکیبی و گسترده را با مثال توضیح دهید.
- اجزای یک سامانهٔ غوطه‌ور (نمایشگر، اپتیک، ردیابی، خط لولهٔ رندر) را با واژگان دقیق نام ببرید.
- بودجهٔ زمان در رندر را تحلیل کنید و دو راه کاهش تأخیر دید‌به‌دست را شرح دهید.
- مسئلهٔ حرکت و بیماری حرکت را توضیح دهید و دو راه‌حل طراحی برای آن پیشنهاد کنید.
- کاربردهای صنعتی و آموزشی را از کاربردهای سرگرمی جدا کنید و معیار ارزیابی هرکدام را بگویید.
- شرطی ترکیبی را به‌کار ببرید و پیامد امروزی یک تصمیم گذشته (و پیامد گذشتهٔ یک وضعیت امروز) را بنویسید.

### 3. Reading / متن اصلی

**عنوان متن: Standing Inside the Data (ایستادن درون داده)**

**[1]** Immersive technology is the family of systems that puts a person inside a picture instead of in front of it. Nazanin is a physiotherapy trainer who uses a virtual rehabilitation room in a clinic, and Kian builds augmented-reality guides for a factory floor. If the field had not been so loud about the metaverse, both of them would be better understood today, because their work has little in common with a virtual party. What they actually build is a training environment that is cheap, repeatable, and measurable.

**[2]** The vocabulary is worth fixing before anything else. Virtual reality replaces the room with a computer-generated one, augmented reality adds objects to the room that is already there, and mixed reality means that the added objects understand the real surface they are placed on, so a virtual valve can hide behind a real pipe. Extended reality is the umbrella term for all three, and spatial computing describes the design habit behind them: the machine works with the room instead of with a window.

**[3]** The interface is a headset, or head mounted display, and the cheap version looks like a heavy pair of goggles. A visor that covers the whole face improves the field of view but isolates the user, while smart glasses leave the face visible and accept a smaller image. The image itself is made by a microdisplay, often a microled panel that is bright enough for daylight, and the light is guided to the eye through a waveguide. Older designs used a stack of fresnel lens elements, which are thin but not sharp, and the newer pancake optics fold the light path so that the headset is shorter and more comfortable. A hologram and a volumetric display try to place the image in the air itself, and a light field records the direction of every ray of light, which is the most ambitious answer of all.

**[4]** Comfort is decided by the parts that nobody photographs. The interpupillary distance must be adjustable, because a wrong setting produces a headache in ten minutes, and eye relief decides whether a user can keep prescription lens in place. A glasses wearer needs either enough space or a corrective insert. A heavy box on the face becomes a neck problem, so a counterweight at the back and a wide head strap are engineering decisions, not accessories. A tethered headset is connected by a cable and has unlimited power; a standalone device is untethered and lives on battery life. Every fleet therefore has a charging dock and a rule about cable management, and every shared device needs a fabric cover that can be cleaned, because sweat, hygiene, and thermal comfort decide whether a user returns for a second session. A cooling fan is not a luxury when a room is full of people.

**[5]** The eye is part of the system. The pupil, the retina, and above all the fovea, the small area of sharp vision, decide how an image must be built, and this is the reason foveated rendering is used: the machine draws the small region that the eye is looking at in high detail and the rest of the picture roughly. The eye also reveals an old conflict that no headset has solved: to look at something close, the eyes turn inwards and the lens changes shape, and a headset forces the first without the second, which is uncomfortable after half an hour.

**[6]** Tracking is the part that customers notice only when it fails. Head tracking keeps the image attached to the world, eye tracking follows the gaze and can also select an item, and hand tracking replaces the controller for simple tasks. Inside out tracking uses cameras on the headset itself, so no external base stations are needed. A gyroscope and an accelerometer, often together with a magnetometer inside one inertial measurement unit, measure rotation and movement between camera frames, which is why a headset can keep working when a hand covers a camera. Six degrees of freedom means that position and rotation are both followed, and three degrees of freedom means that only rotation is followed.

**[7]** A device that does not know the room is useless for training. Simultaneous localization and mapping builds a map while it estimates the position inside it, and a marker or an anchor puts a virtual object on a real point. Lidar and a depth sensor measure the space, and the result is a point cloud that the system turns into simple surfaces. Occlusion is what makes a scene believable: a virtual object must be hidden by a real chair in front of it. Passthrough, which shows the real world through cameras, is the feature that turns a virtual headset into an augmented one.

**[8]** Rendering has a budget, and the budget is measured in time. The render pipeline must deliver a new frame for each eye, often ninety times a second, and the delay between a head movement and the matching picture is the motion to photon latency. When the budget is missed, the user feels it: judder is the regular stutter of a missed rhythm, stutter is irregular, and a dropped frame is a visible jump. Two tricks hide the gap. Reprojection reuses the previous frame with a small correction, and warp bends an almost-finished image into the new head position. Predictive tracking guesses where the head will be in twenty milliseconds, which is honest only if the guess is close.

**[9]** The content itself is a pile of numbers. A polygon and a vertex define a surface, a texture paints it, and a shader decides how the light behaves on it. A voxel is the three-dimensional version of a pixel, and it is used when a scene is built from a volume instead of from a skin. A normal map adds small bumps without adding geometry, tone mapping controls brightness, motion blur hides a fast movement, and antialiasing softens the staircase on every edge. Because a headset has a hard frame limit, the artist must use level of detail, culling, and a careful draw call budget, and the fastest scenes are those that use ray tracing and global illumination instead of baked lighting.

**[10]** Building a scene is a production process rather than a programming task. Motion capture records a real actor, retargeting moves that movement onto a different rig, and the rig is the skeleton that an asset is built on. World building is a separate skill, procedural generation fills a large world with detail that nobody drew by hand, and a physics engine decides how objects fall. A trainer who ignores this pipeline produces a demonstration; a trainer who respects it produces a course.

**[11]** The reason people pay for all of this is presence: the feeling of being somewhere else, which grows from immersion, from a believable body, and from agency. Embodiment is the technical version of the same idea, and telepresence is the workplace version, in which a person is present at a distance through a machine. Presence cannot be measured directly, so a serious project measures the things that create it: field of view, latency, comfort, and the number of times the user forgets the controller.

**[12]** A metaverse is the social version of the same word. It is a virtual world that keeps existing when a user leaves, and a persistent world is the technical promise behind it. A lobby is where a session starts, and a mature platform adds matchmaking, social presence, and proximity chat, so that a voice is heard only by the people who are standing near. The word itself is often used as marketing, and a project that cannot explain the difference between a world and a room will not survive its first budget review.

**[13]** Movement inside a scene is a design problem. Haptics adds touch to the picture, force feedback pushes back against the hand, and a tactile display sends a small pattern to the skin. A haptic glove is the most ambitious version, and an exoskeleton goes further and moves the user's own hand. Everyday interaction is simpler: a controller or a tracking controller with a pointer, a hand gesture, a pinch to select, and a ray cast from the hand to a distant object.

**[14]** The hardest problem in the field is locomotion, because the inner ear is not convinced by a picture. A teleport moves the user instantly and is comfortable but breaks the sense of space; a snap turn rotates in fixed steps; a smooth turn is natural and makes some users sick within a minute. This is why every serious application offers a comfort mode, and why the first design review is about movement rather than about graphics.

**[15]** Safety is a physical question, not a policy. A play space is the real area that a user is allowed to occupy, and a guardian system or chaperone draws its border before anyone walks into a wall. Cybersickness is the name of the illness that follows a bad design, and it appears as nausea, dizziness, or a headache long before a user complains. Eye strain and a long session length are the quieter problems, and a clinic that ignores hygiene will lose its staff after the first shared headset. A shared device must be kept sanitary between users, and the oldest rule in the room is still the best one: stop before the user feels unwell.

**[16]** The ethical questions follow the hardware. A camera on a headset is a sensor in a private room, and a network of headsets becomes a form of surveillance whether the owner intends it or not. Eye tracking adds data privacy to the same list, because a gaze can reveal attention, arousal, and habits. Addiction, harassment, and avatar misconduct are social problems inside the world, and physical safety is the oldest problem outside it. Neither list is solved by a better lens.

**[17]** Industry uses the same tools for narrower and more useful purposes. A digital twin is a model of a real machine, and an engineer can walk around it, which makes a prototype review cheaper than a physical mock-up. Teleoperation lets a skilled person work at a distance, and remote assistance puts an expert's hands into a young technician's view. In the best deployments the software measures the result: a trainer with an assessment rubric can say which trainee is ready, and a factory can compare the error rate before and after the guide.

**[18]** Training research has one important lesson for this field, and it is not about graphics. Transfer of training is the question of whether a skill learned in a headset is available in the real world, and it is measured with a retention test a month later. A short, uncomfortable session can produce a good score on the same day and no skill a month later. If the clinic had measured only the immediate result, it would still be buying headsets for a course that teaches nothing.

**[19]** Consumer applications are louder and shallower. A virtual museum, a tourism tour, a real estate tour, and a virtual try-on all work well enough to sell a product, and a live event, a concert, or a sports broadcast fills a stadium with people who are sitting at home. A flight simulator and a surgical training system are older examples of the same idea, and they survived because the alternative is expensive. The pattern is consistent: the technology wins where practice is expensive, dangerous, or impossible, and it loses where a screen is already good enough.

**[20]** Nazanin's clinic bought four headsets for stroke rehabilitation, and her first design was a copy of a computer game. The patients enjoyed it, which was the good news, and half of them stopped after eight minutes, which was the real news. If she had read the comfort literature first, she would not have put a smooth turn and a fast corridor into the first version.

**[21]** Her second version changed the movement rather than the graphics. A comfort mode reduced the field of view during any rotation, a teleport replaced the corridor, and the session length was cut to six minutes with a break. Nausea reports fell to almost nothing, and if the headset had been lighter, the older patients would have managed ten minutes by the third week. The lesson she repeats to every new physiotherapist is that immersion is a tool and not a goal.

**[22]** Her third problem was measurement, and it was harder than the engineering. A rehabilitation score is not a game score, and she needed an assessment rubric that a colleague could apply without arguing. She recorded the time to complete a movement, the number of errors, and the subjective difficulty. If she were less patient with paperwork, the clinic would have had four expensive toys instead of a course with a before-and-after number.

**[23]** The result was published as a short report, and her conclusion was careful. If the same training had been done on a screen, the immediate scores would have been similar, and the difference appeared in the third week, when the patients who trained in the headset kept practising at home. Whether that difference would survive a year is still unknown.

**[24]** Kian's project has a different shape. A factory assembles one machine in many configurations, and a new technician needs six weeks to learn the wiring. His augmented-reality guide puts a step-by-step instruction into the technician's own view, and a digital twin of the machine sits behind it. The pilot was measured on one number: the time to find the correct connector.

**[25]** His technical problem was occlusion. The first version drew the virtual cable over the real one, so the technician could not see the difference between the instruction and the equipment. The fix was a depth sensor and a passthrough stream that let the system hide the virtual object behind the real surface. If augmented reality could not solve occlusion, the guide would be a poster with extra steps.

**[26]** His incident was a latency problem that no test had predicted. During a fast head movement, the drawn label arrived after the technician had already looked away, and on one occasion he connected the wrong pair. Nobody was hurt, and the report was written anyway. If the frame rate had been stable, that mistake would not have happened, and if the team had tested the guide in a real glove instead of a clean room, they would have found the problem earlier.

**[27]** The second version was a compromise between quality and delay. Foveated rendering cut the cost of each frame, predictive tracking filled the gap during a fast movement, and reprojection kept the last image in the right place. The measured latency fell below the point at which a technician notices it, and the error rate in the pilot line fell with it.

**[28]** What Nazanin and Kian share is a habit rather than a technology. Both of them measure a real task, both of them keep a comfort rule, and neither of them trusts a demonstration. If the field had more of this habit, it would have fewer press releases and more products, and if a vendor were honest about the limits of a headset, the customer's first pilot would be shorter and cheaper.

**[29]** The market side of the story is less inspiring. A killer app is always expected and rarely named, the adoption curve in industry is slower than the curve in gaming, and device fragmentation means that one team must support three headsets with three toolkits. An open standard is the answer that everybody supports in a conference and delays in a roadmap. A content library, an exclusive title, and a subscription model matter to a consumer platform, and cost per seat matters to a hospital: the same technology is judged by a completely different number in the two rooms.

**[30]** So what should a student learn from this field? Learn the human first, because the eye, the inner ear, and the neck are not optional parts of the specification. Learn to measure a task instead of a feeling, and learn to write a rubric before the pilot starts. Then learn the pipeline, because a scene that cannot hold a frame rate is not a product. A student who can do those three things will be useful in a hospital, a factory, and a studio.

**[31]** A short checklist is worth keeping beside the keyboard. What is the field of view, what is the motion to photon latency, what is the session length that a user can tolerate, and what is the number that proves the training worked? If a project cannot answer those four questions, it does not matter how beautiful the world is.

**[32]** Health guidance belongs in the same document as the design. A first session should be short, a user should sit down if nausea begins, a break should be scheduled rather than requested, and a shared device should be cleaned between users. None of this is dramatic, and all of it decides whether a clinic's staff will still be using the system in six months.

**[33]** The last question is the honest one. If a screen had been good enough, the headset would not be needed, and if the training were dangerous in reality, the headset would be essential. A serious project answers that question in one paragraph, and everything else — the optics, the pipeline, the rubric — follows from the answer. Presence is a means; the skill that a patient or a technician carries out of the room is the end.

**ب) نکات ترجمه**

۱. **شرطی ترکیبی را با دو زمان فارسی برگردانید:** *If the clinic had measured the task, the course would be different today* ← «اگر درمانگاه کار را اندازه گرفته بود، دوره امروز طور دیگری بود». زمان بند دوم حال است، چون نتیجه در حال رخ می‌دهد.
۲. **جهت دوم را هم بشناسید:** *If the rubric were clearer, the trainees would not have complained last month* ← «اگر شیوه‌نامه روشن‌تر بود، کارآموزان ماه گذشته شکایت نمی‌کردند». شرط در حال است و نتیجه در گذشته.
۳. **قیدهای زمان، راهنمای ترجمه‌اند:** وجود *now*, *today*, *at the moment* نشان می‌دهد نتیجه در حال است و وجود *last term*, *yesterday*, *in 2023* نشان می‌دهد نتیجه در گذشته رخ داده است.
۴. **«اگر» فارسی همیشه کافی نیست:** برای تأکید می‌توانید از «اگر ... نبود» یا «به لطف ...» استفاده کنید: *If it had not been for the trainer, the course would be shorter today* ← «اگر مربی نبود، دوره امروز کوتاه‌تر بود».
۵. **واژه‌های فنی را با معادل دقیق بیاورید:** *to render* ← «رندر کردن»، *to track* ← «ردیابی کردن»، *to calibrate* ← «کالیبره کردن»، *to teleport* ← «جهش مکانی کردن».
۶. **واحدها را به فارسی بنویسید:** «نود هرتز»، «بیست میلی‌ثانیه»، «شش درجهٔ آزادی»؛ عدد لاتین داخل متن فارسی نگذارید.
۷. **سرواژه‌ها را یکدست نگه دارید:** HMD، SLAM، AR، VR و XR را ترجمه نکنید؛ در نخستین کاربرد معادل فارسی را در پرانتز بیاورید.
۸. **از این ترجمه‌های تحت‌اللفظی بپرهیزید:** *pancake optics* ← «اپتیک پنکیکی» (نه «اپتیک خوراکی»)، *killer app* ← «کاربرد قاتل» (نه «برنامهٔ کشنده»)، *screen door effect* ← «اثر توری» (نه «اثر درِ صفحه»).

### 7. Grammar Focus / نکتهٔ گرامری

**موضوع: شرطی‌های ترکیبی (mixed conditionals)**

**توضیح فارسی:**

شرطی ترکیبی یعنی شرط و نتیجه به **دو زمان مختلف** تعلق دارند. نوع دوم به حال خیالی و نوع سوم به گذشتهٔ خیالی مربوط است؛ شرطی ترکیبی، این دو را با هم می‌آمیزد و دو الگو دارد.

**الگوی ۱ — شرط در گذشته، نتیجه در حال:** `if + had + p.p.` و `would + فعل ساده` به‌همراه قید حال (*now, today, at the moment*).
*If the clinic had measured the task, the course would be different today.*
*If she had trained in the headset, she would be faster now.*

**الگوی ۲ — شرط در حال، نتیجه در گذشته:** `if + گذشتهٔ ساده` و `would have + p.p.` به‌همراه قید گذشته (*last month, yesterday, in 2023*).
*If the rubric were clearer, the trainees would not have complained last month.*
*If the headset were lighter, the older patients would have managed ten minutes.*

**نشانه‌های زمانی، راهنمای اصلی هستند:** قید حاضر در بند نتیجه، الگوی ۱ را می‌طلبد و قید گذشته، الگوی ۲ را. به‌جای *would have* می‌توان *could have* یا *might have* گذاشت: *If the trainer had written the rubric, the clinic could have compared the two courses.*

دو خطای رایج فارسی‌زبانان: ① آوردن *would have* در بند شرط ✗ *If the clinic would have measured the task, ...* ← درست: *If the clinic had measured the task, ...*؛ ② هم‌زمان نکردن دو بند ✗ *If the rubric were clearer, they would not complain last month* ← درست: *... they would not have complained last month.*

**جدول ۱ — دو الگوی شرطی ترکیبی:**

| الگو | بند شرط | بند نتیجه | نمونه |
|---|---|---|---|
| گذشته ← حال | if + had + p.p. | would + فعل ساده | If she had measured the task, she would be more confident today. |
| حال ← گذشته | if + گذشتهٔ ساده | would have + p.p. | If the rubric were clearer, they would not have complained last month. |

**جدول ۲ — قید زمان تصمیم می‌گیرد:**

| قید در بند نتیجه | الگو | نمونه |
|---|---|---|
| now / today / at the moment | گذشته ← حال | If the headset had been lighter, the patients would manage ten minutes now. |
| last week / yesterday / in 2023 | حال ← گذشته | If the comfort mode were standard, the clinic would not have lost those users. |
| *(بدون قید)* | از معنا پیروی کنید | If he had tested the guide, the mistake would not have happened. |

**جدول ۳ — مقایسه و خطاهای رایج:**

| ساخت | نمونه | نکته |
|---|---|---|
| نوع دوم (حال) | If the headset were lighter, it would cost more. | هر دو بند در حال |
| نوع سوم (گذشته) | If the headset had been lighter, it would have cost more. | هر دو بند در گذشته |
| شرطی ترکیبی | If the headset had been lighter, the patients would manage ten minutes now. | دو زمان مختلف |
| ✗ خطای رایج | If the clinic would have measured the task, ... | پس از *if* فعل وجهی نمی‌آید |

**پنج مثال از دنیای کامپیوتر:**

**موضوع: A Decision That Still Affects Me — تصمیمی که هنوز اثرش مانده است**

یک تصمیم واقعی را انتخاب کنید که **خودتان** گرفته‌اید و اثرش هنوز باقی است: انتخاب یک رشته یا درس، خرید یک وسیله، عادت مطالعه، یا شروع یک پروژه. سپس دربارهٔ آن بنویسید:

- آن تصمیم چه بود و در چه وضعیتی گرفته شد؟
- نتیجهٔ آن **امروز** چیست؟
- اگر آن تصمیم را نگرفته بودید، امروز چه چیزی فرق می‌کرد؟ (الگوی گذشته ← حال)
- کدام وضعیت امروز شما نتیجهٔ یک اتفاق گذشته است؟ (همین الگو، از زاویهٔ دیگر)
- چه چیزی در حال شما وجود دارد که باعث شد آن اتفاق گذشته رخ دهد؟ (الگوی حال ← گذشته)
- یک جملهٔ محتاطانه بنویسید که فرض خودتان را محدود کند.

**شرایط نگارش:**

- نوشته بین **۲۰۰ تا ۳۰۰ واژه** باشد.
- دست‌کم **۴ جملهٔ شرطی ترکیبی** داشته باشد: دو مورد با الگوی گذشته ← حال و دو مورد با الگوی حال ← گذشته.
- دست‌کم یک جمله با *if it had not been for* در متن باشد.
- دست‌کم **۲ اصطلاح ربطی** از این فهرست به‌کار ببرید: *however*, *therefore*, *even so*, *as a result*.
- دست‌کم **۸ واژه** از جدول‌های واژگان بخش ۲ استفاده شود.

### 10. Mini Project / پروژهٔ کوچک

**انشا (نیمه‌تحقیقی): یک تمرین که ارزش واقعیت مجازی را دارد**

یک مهارت عملی را انتخاب کنید که **در محیط خودتان** آموزش داده می‌شود (تزریق و بانداژ در درمانگاه، سیم‌کشی یک دستگاه در کارگاه، کار با یک آزمایشگاه شیمی، یا آماده‌سازی یک نمایشگاه) و طرح یک دورهٔ تمرینی غوطه‌ور بنویسید:

- **مهارت و کاربر:** چه کسی تمرین می‌کند و در پایان چه کاری باید بتواند انجام دهد؟
- **چرا تمرین واقعی سخت است؟** گران، خطرناک، یا ناممکن — با یک عدد توضیح دهید.
- **شکل دستی:** چرخهٔ تمرین را روی کاغذ بکشید: آموزش ← تمرین در هدست ← بازخورد ← تمرین واقعی ← ارزیابی پس از یک ماه. روی هر پیکان یک معیار بنویسید.
- **جدول دستی مقایسه:** جدولی با چهار سطر بکشید و تمرین واقعی را با تمرین غوطه‌ور مقایسه کنید: هزینه، خطر، تکرارپذیری، زمان مربی.
- **دو عدد موفقیت:** یک عدد پیش از دوره و یک عدد پس از آن را نام ببرید و بگویید چگونه اندازه‌گیری می‌شوند.
- **قاعدهٔ آسودگی:** یک قاعدهٔ روشن برای طول نشست و توقف بنویسید.
- **جمله‌های گرامری:** پنج جملهٔ شرطی ترکیبی بنویسید، شامل هر دو الگو.

**شرایط نگارش:**

- گزارش را **دست‌نویس روی کاغذ** بنویسید و در **جلسهٔ بعد** به مدرس تحویل دهید.
- دست‌کم **۲۵۰ واژه** باشد.
- دست‌کم **۵ جملهٔ شرطی ترکیبی** (هر دو الگو) و **۲ جمله** با *might* یا *could* داشته باشد.
- طرح دستی چرخهٔ تمرین و جدول دستی مقایسه در گزارش باشد.
- دست‌کم **۱۲ واژه** از جدول‌های واژگان بخش ۲ در گزارش به‌کار رفته باشد.

**واژه‌های یاری‌دهنده:** headset · field of view · frame rate · motion to photon latency · comfort mode · play space · session length · presence · digital twin · teleoperation · assessment rubric · trainer

### 11. Did You Know? / آیا می‌دانستید؟

1. **The word "metaverse" comes from a 1992 science-fiction novel, long before it became a business plan.**
   واژهٔ «متاورس» از رمانی علمی‌تخیلی در سال ۱۹۹۲ می‌آید؛ مدت‌ها پیش از آنکه به یک برنامهٔ تجاری تبدیل شود.

2. **The first head-mounted display was demonstrated in 1968, and it was so heavy that it had to be hung from the ceiling.**
   نخستین نمایشگر سرنصب در سال ۱۹۶۸ نمایش داده شد و آن‌قدر سنگین بود که باید از سقف آویزان می‌شد.

3. **The term augmented reality was used in 1990 by a researcher at an aircraft manufacturer, who wanted to replace paper instructions on an assembly line.**
   اصطلاح «واقعیت افزوده» در سال ۱۹۹۰ توسط پژوهشگری در یک سازندهٔ هواپیما به کار رفت که می‌خواست دستورهای کاغذی خط تولید را جایگزین کند.

4. **Research on early head-mounted displays in the 1990s reported a comfort threshold of about twenty milliseconds for motion to photon latency.**
   پژوهش روی نخستین نمایشگرهای سرنصب در دههٔ ۱۹۹۰ آستانهٔ آسودگی را حدود بیست میلی‌ثانیه برای تأخیر دید‌به‌دست گزارش کرد.

5. **Simulator sickness was documented in military flight simulators in the 1970s, before any consumer headset existed.**
   بیماری شبیه‌ساز در دههٔ ۱۹۷۰ در شبیه‌سازهای پرواز نظامی مستند شد؛ پیش از آنکه هیچ هدست مصرفی وجود داشته باشد.

6. **Two consumer headsets reached the market in 2016, and within three years the entry price of the category had fallen by about half.**
   دو هدست مصرفی در سال ۲۰۱۶ به بازار رسیدند و در کمتر از سه سال، قیمت ورودی این دسته حدود نصف شد.

7. **In 2021, one large company renamed itself around the metaverse and invested billions, and industry reports in 2023 described very large losses in that division.**
   در سال ۲۰۲۱ شرکتی بزرگ نام خود را حول «متاورس» تغییر داد و میلیاردها سرمایه‌گذاری کرد؛ گزارش‌های صنعتی در ۲۰۲۳ از زیان‌های بسیار سنگین آن بخش خبر دادند.

8. **Eye tracking was used in reading research in the early twentieth century, decades before it appeared in a headset.**
   ردیابی چشم در اوایل قرن بیستم در پژوهش خواندن به کار می‌رفت؛ ده‌ها سال پیش از آنکه در هدست‌ها ظاهر شود.
